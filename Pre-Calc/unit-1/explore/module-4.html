Two Photos

A camera reads your license plate as you get on the freeway at 3:00. Another reads it as you get off, 30 miles later, at 3:20. Nobody ever measured your speed. The limit is 65. So what can two photos actually prove?

Cut up the trip. Then try the speedometer.
First The Crowd

Cut the trip into pieces and average their speeds. Then try glancing at the speedometer instead.

The cameras only saw two moments, so all they can work out is an average: 30 miles in 20 minutes is 90 miles an hour — the dashed blue line. The gold curve is one trip that fits both photos: stuck at the on-ramp light, flooring it, cruising, getting off. Cut it into pieces and each bar underneath is one speed. Piece by piece, it’s how far that piece went over how long it took — the slope of its line. Speedometer glances, it’s what the speedometer said at the start of that piece. The white line is the average of the bars. Every one of those speeds is listed under the chart — then added up and divided.

What you just saw

Average each piece’s speed — how far it went over how long it took — and you land on 90 exactly, however many pieces: the little distances add up to 30 miles and the little times add up to 20 minutes, so nothing else can come out. Average speedometer glances instead and you miss — one glance at 3:00 says 0, because you were sitting at the light. Glance more often and the average creeps toward 90, but it never quite gets there. A glance tells you how fast you were going at one moment. A piece tells you how far you actually got.

The names for this, when you’re ready for them: each piece’s speed is its average rate of change — the slope of the secant line across it, (s(b) − s(a)) ÷ (b − a). Adding up the little distances is a telescoping sum — every in-between moment is added once and taken away once, so only the two photos survive. And a plain average of speeds only works when every piece lasts the same amount of time; otherwise you need a weighted average, counting each piece by how long it lasted. It’s why driving somewhere at 30 and back at 60 averages 40 miles an hour, not 45.

Then The Ticket

A week later, a ticket shows up in the mail.

It says“At some moment, your speedometer read exactly 90 mph.”

The cameras proved your average: 90 miles an hour, a long way over 65. This claims something else — something about one moment, and the cameras never saw a single moment in between. Is the ticket right anyway? Say what you think, and why.

Two photos can’t tell you how fast you were going. They can tell you your average — and sometimes that’s all anyone needs.
