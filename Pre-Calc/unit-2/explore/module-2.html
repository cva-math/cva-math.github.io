What you just saw

A multiplication comes out zero only when one of the pieces is zero — so the curve can meet the axis at −4, −1 and 2, and nowhere else. Those are the only three numbers that switch a piece off. Everywhere else, the pieces decide which side the graph is on by how many of them are negative: an odd number of minus signs puts it below the axis, an even number puts it above. Slide past a zero and exactly one piece changes sign, so the graph changes sides. Unless you wrote that piece twice — then two of them change sign at the same moment, the two minus signs cancel, and the graph comes back the way it came. Write it three times and three signs flip, which is odd again, so it crosses after all — flattening as it goes through.

The names for this, when you’re ready for them: a number c with f(c) = 0 is a zero of f, and (x − c) is a factor of it. You never get one without the other, and that is the Factor Theorem — which is why you can test a factor without dividing anything: work out f(c) and see whether it is 0. How many copies of a factor you write is that zero’s multiplicity, normally written as an exponent: (x + 1)(x + 1) is (x + 1)2. Odd multiplicity crosses, even multiplicity touches and turns back. And the copies are what run up the degree — so a polynomial of degree n is a product of at most n pieces, and its graph has at most n places it can meet the axis.

Slide as far as you like along the number line: this graph never meets the axis. No x makes it zero, so by everything above it has no zeros — and so no factors. But if it could be written as two pieces multiplied together, what would they have to be? Say what you think, and why.

A factored polynomial is a multiplication, and a zero is one piece switching off. Nothing else can put the graph on the axis.
