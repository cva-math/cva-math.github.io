Almost Nothing

A rational function is one polynomial divided by another. Every function on this page divides by the same thing, x − 4 — and every one of the three experiments is about something getting close to nothing.

Step closer. Go far out. Step closer again.
Act I The Wall

Step toward x = 4, where the bottom is zero. Watch the top, the bottom, and what one divided by the other comes to.

The gold curve is f(x) = (3x + 2) ÷ (x − 4), and the dashed line is x = 4, where its bottom is zero. Pick a side and slide toward it — 1 away, then 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001 — and the table keeps every value you land on. Come at it from both sides. Then land right on x = 4.

What you just saw

Near x = 4 the top hardly moves — it’s always about 14 — but the bottom keeps shrinking toward zero, and dividing by something tiny makes something huge. Ten times closer, ten times bigger: 143, then 1,403, then 14,003. From the right the bottom is a tiny positive number, so the graph shoots up; from the left it’s a tiny negative number, so the graph plunges. And at x = 4 itself there’s nothing to work out, because 14 can’t be divided by zero. The graph runs up one side of the line and down the other, and never touches it.

The names for this, when you’re ready for them: the line x = 4 is a vertical asymptote. Each side of it is a one-sided limit. limx → 4+f(x) = ∞ says that coming from the right, f(x) climbs without end, and limx → 4−f(x) = −∞ says that coming from the left, it falls without end. The little + and − on the 4 say which side you came from — and the table has them now. ∞ isn’t a value f ever reaches. It’s a direction: this way, forever.

What you just saw

Far out, the small terms on the top and the bottom stop mattering — the same thing that happened two weeks ago — so the fraction behaves like its two highest-power terms, one over the other. With 3x + 2 on top, that’s 3x over x, which is just 3: the graph settles on 3, both ways. With only a 2 on top, the bottom has the higher power: it keeps growing while the top stays put, so the fraction shrinks toward 0. With 3x2 + 2 on top, the top has the higher power: 3x2 over x is 3x, which never stops growing, so the graph never settles at all.

The names for this: the line a graph settles on is a horizontal asymptote — y = 3 for the middle top, y = 0 for the first. Settling is a limit too: limx → ∞f(x) = 3 and limx → −∞f(x) = 3, and the table has them for whichever top you pick. The same power on top and bottom: it settles on the ratio of the numbers in front. A higher power on the bottom: it settles on 0. A higher power on top: it doesn’t settle.

What you just saw

This time the top shrinks too. At x = 4 the top is zero as well as the bottom — and a top that’s zero at 4 has a factor of x − 4, which was last week. So the top is (3x + 2)(x − 4). The two tiny numbers shrink together, so their ratio never blows up: it closes in on 14, which is 3(4) + 2 — the same 14 the top was heading for in Act I. The x − 4 on top cancels the one on the bottom, and what’s left is the straight line 3x + 2, with one point missing. At x = 4 itself, 0 ÷ 0 still isn’t a number.

The names for this: the missing point is a hole, at (4, 14). The limit is there even though the value isn’t: limx → 4f(x) = 14 from both sides, while f(4) doesn’t exist at all. Which is why the bottom being zero only tells you where to look. Factor the top and the bottom before you decide what happens there.

Then The Line It Settles On

One more, and the page won’t answer this one for you.

Settles on y = 1, both waysf(x) = x2 + 3xx2 + 1

Its bottom is never zero, so it has no walls at all, and far out in both directions it settles on y = 1. A graph can never cross a wall — at the wall there’s no value to cross through. Can it cross the line it settles on? Say what you think, and why.

Near a zero of the bottom, the graph runs off forever — unless the top is almost nothing too. Far out, the small terms are almost nothing, and the biggest powers decide.
