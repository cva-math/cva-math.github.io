Four Ways Out

Up close, a polynomial’s graph can wander all over — over bumps, into dips, across the axis and back. Zoom out far enough, though, and it leaves the screen. There are only four ways out.

          What you just saw

          Every card went where it did because of one term — the one with the highest power. Whether that power is even or odd decides whether the two ends agree: even sends them the same way, odd sends them opposite ways. Its sign decides the right-hand end: positive leaves up, negative leaves down. Everything else on the card only shapes the middle. And that term doesn’t have to be written first: it can turn up anywhere in the line, or be split up across factors — where it’s the x part of every factor multiplied together, and a squared factor counts twice.

          The names for this, when you’re ready for them: which way a graph leaves is its end behavior. The term with the highest power is the leading term — its power is the polynomial’s degree, and its number is the leading coefficient. And every corner is a pair of limits. Down, then up is written limx → −∞f(x) = −∞ and limx → ∞f(x) = ∞: as x runs off to the left, f(x) falls without end, and as it runs off to the right, it climbs without end. Your four corners have their limits now. ∞ isn’t a number f(x) ever gets to. It’s a direction: this way, forever.
