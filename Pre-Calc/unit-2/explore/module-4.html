Two Doors

A graph that’s above the x‑axis can only get below it two ways: down through the axis, at a zero — or across a gap, at a break. Find the doors, and you can solve the whole thing without ever seeing the graph.

Test in the dark. Shut a door. Catch a fake.
Act I In the Dark

Where is this graph above the x‑axis? You can’t see it. Test numbers to find out.

The graph is hidden. All you know is where its doors are: the top is zero at −3 and 1 (the circles), and the bottom is zero at −1 and 4 (the dashed lines). Tap anywhere on the chart to test a number — the page works out f(x) there and shows you which side of the axis it’s on, and the strip underneath keeps a + or a − for every test. Test every piece between the doors, try a second number in one of them, and then lift the fog.

What you just saw

Every number you tested between the same two doors landed on the same side. That’s not luck. To get from above the axis to below it, the graph has to go down through the axis — a zero — or jump a gap where it has no value at all — a break. Between two doors it can do neither, so its sign can’t change there. One test tells you the whole piece, and you never needed to see the graph.

The names for this, when you’re ready for them: the doors cut the number line into intervals, one test value in each one gives its sign, and the strip you built is a sign chart. Solving f(x) > 0 is then just reading off the + pieces: (−∞, −3) ∪ (−1, 1) ∪ (4, ∞). That’s interval notation, with round brackets because the doors themselves never make f(x) greater than 0. Five pieces, five tests. The graph only confirms what the tests already said.

Act II The Door That Stays Shut

Write (x + 3) twice. Then change the inequality, and watch the answer on the axis.

The same function as Act I, with the fog gone. The strip keeps the sign of every piece, and the blue on the x‑axis is the answer to the inequality you pick: a filled dot is a point that’s in the answer, a hollow one is a point that isn’t. Try all four inequalities with (x + 3) written once, then written twice — and watch x = −3.

What you just saw

Written twice, (x + 3) still makes f zero at −3, but both copies change sign there at the same moment — and two sign changes cancel. The graph touches the axis and goes back the way it came, so the pieces on both sides of −3 have the same sign. It’s a door that stays shut. And that changes the answers in small, sharp ways. A zero is in the answer exactly when the inequality says “or equal”; a break never is, because there’s no value there to compare. So with ≥, −3 is in the answer all by itself, one lonely point. With <, the answer runs right up to −3 from both sides and skips it.

The names for this: a factor written an even number of times has even multiplicity, and its zero touches without switching — so on a sign chart you test both sides of every door, and never assume. In interval notation a single point goes in braces, {−3}, and ∪, the union, joins the pieces: {−3} ∪ (−1, 1] ∪ (4, ∞). A square bracket includes its end and a round one doesn’t; ∞ always gets a round one. A break is never in the answer, whatever the inequality says. There’s nothing there.

What you just saw

Multiplying both sides of an equation by (x − 1) is safe everywhere except one place: x = 1, where (x − 1) is zero. Anything times zero is zero, so at x = 1 the two new sides agree no matter what the old ones were doing — and the new equation picks up a solution the old one never had. You can see it: on the right, the line and the curve really do meet at x = 1. On the left there’s nothing there to meet. Both sides run up and down the same wall, never touching. Only x = 2 is real.

The names for this: an answer the algebra hands you that doesn’t solve the original equation is an extraneous solution. Multiplying by a bottom can only ever add one where that bottom is zero — at a break — so the check is short: put every answer back into the bottoms of the original, and throw out any that make one zero. Two curves can only meet where both of them exist.

Then The Claim

One more, and the page won’t answer this one for you.

The whole week, in one sentence“A graph can only switch sides of the x‑axis at a zero or a break.”

Every inequality on this page leaned on that sentence, and so does every sign chart you’ll ever make. Is it always true? Could any graph get from above the axis to below it without a zero or a break? Say what you think, and why.

Above or below, a graph can only change sides at a zero or a break. Find the doors, test once between each, and check every answer against the bottoms.
