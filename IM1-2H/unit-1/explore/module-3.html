The Point That Won’t Move

Last week you found where two lines cross by looking. Looking gets you about there. This is how you get it exactly — and why the moves for doing it are not the tricks they look like.

What you just saw

Every single line you drew went through the same point. It has to: that point is the one pair of numbers that makes both equations true, so it makes any pile of them true as well — add them, double one, subtract three of the other, it survives all of it. Which means you are free to pick whichever combination you like. And two of them are worth picking, because two of them come out with a letter missing, and a line with only one letter in it hands you a number.

The names for this, when you’re ready for them: a new equation built by scaling and adding the old ones is a linear combination of them, and the whole method of hunting for the one that drops a variable is elimination. The two you found were k = 2, giving 5x = 20, and k = −3, giving 5y = 15. This is also why multiplying an equation through by a number is allowed and changes nothing you care about: 2x − 2y = 2 is the same line as x − y = 1, drawn by the same points.

What you just saw

Standing on ② is what let you stop thinking about ②. Once y is written as x − 1, equation ① stops having two letters in it and becomes one thing that climbs as you walk — and you are just waiting for it to reach 18. The crossing is where it does. Same point as Act I, found by walking rather than by adding.

The name: replacing a letter with what it equals, and solving what’s left, is substitution. So you have two ways in, and the system tells you which one it wants. A variable already sitting alone — y = x − 1, p = 2m + 10 — is asking to be substituted. Matching or opposite coefficients — two equations that both start 2x, or one with +3y against one with −3y — is asking to be eliminated. Neither is the right method. The one that gets you there in fewer steps is.

Then The Card

One system the page won’t solve for you — and you have met it before.

Two ways out of the same graduating class
  1. 60000t − y = 262500 the nurse — four years of school, then $60,000 a year
  2. 38000t − y = 41000 the HVAC tech — one year, then $38,000 a year
These are the two lines from this unit’s trailer, written out. There you found where they cross by dragging until it looked right — about year ten. Now get it exactly.

Look at the two equations before you pick a method. One of them takes a single step, and the other takes several. Which one is the system asking for, and how do you know before you start?

Two lines cross once, and nothing you do to the equations can shake that point loose. Every method is just a different way of walking up to it.
