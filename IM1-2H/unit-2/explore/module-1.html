Six Questions

Somewhere on this page is a phone’s whole day — every minute of its battery, from breakfast to bed. You can’t see it. You can only name a moment and ask, and it answers with a number. Here the graph is hidden, so asking is the only way to see anything.

Ask well. You only get six.
Act I The Low Point

A phone, from 7 a.m. to 11 p.m. When did its battery run lowest — and how low? You get six questions.

Each question is B(t) for a t you choose, where t counts hours after 7 a.m. So B(3) asks for the battery at 10 a.m. The page answers, plants the point on the chart, and writes the statement down. Spend them well — once all six are gone, the day shows itself.

What you just saw

Every question you asked put one point on the chart — and when the day showed itself, every one of your points was sitting on it. That’s not luck. The curve is just the answer to every question you could have asked, all at once: a point for every moment. You asked about six moments. Wherever the low point or the lunchtime charge fell between two of your questions, you had no way to see it.

The names for this, when you’re ready for them: B is the function — the rule that takes a time and gives back a battery level. t is the input, and B(t), read “B of t,” is the output. So B(12.5) = 11 says: put in 12.5, get out 11. It is not B times 12.5 — the parentheses hold the question. And every statement B(a) = b is one point, (a, b), on the graph. Asking a function a question and reading its graph are the same act.

Act II Same Six Answers

Dani swears her phone had the exact same day as yours. At all six of your times, she’s right. Is it the same day?

Her phone is D. At every one of your six times, D gave the same answer B did — they’re the rings on the chart. Ask D anything you like, as many times as you like, and find out whether she’s right. Your phone’s day is drawn in gold.

Ask your six questions in Act I first — this act uses them.

What you just saw

Dani’s phone agreed with yours at every one of your six times, and it still had a different day. Six matching answers pinned down six matching points — and said nothing at all about the hours in between, which is where hers went its own way. Two functions can give the same output at every input you tried and still not be the same function.

The name: two functions are equal only when they give the same output for every input — every time of day, not six of them. B(t) = D(t) at your six times was true. B = D never was. A list of statements about a function tells you exactly what it says, point by point, and nothing about the places it doesn’t mention.

How many is enough?

Suppose both phones drained in a perfectly straight line.

How many matching answers would it take to prove they had the same day? And for real phones, is any number of questions ever enough?

Say what you think — and what it is about a straight line that makes it different.

A function answers every question you could ever ask it, and its graph is all of those answers at once. Six questions get you six points. The day happens in between.
