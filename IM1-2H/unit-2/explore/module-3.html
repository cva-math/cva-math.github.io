Wrong Way Round

Solve an inequality the way you’d solve an equation — same moves, same order — and you get an answer that looks exactly right. This one looks exactly right. Don’t trust it until a number has checked it.

Test it before you trust it.
Act I Test It

50 GB of data left this month, and you want to keep more than 20 GB. How many hours of video can you watch?

Say an hour of HD video uses 3 GB. After h hours you have 50 − 3h GB left, so the question is 50 − 3h > 20. Here it is, solved the way you’d solve an equation:

50 − 3h > 20the question −3h > −30subtract 50 from both sides h > 10divide both sides by −3

More than 10 hours. It looks right. Now test it — drag h and let go, and the value stays on the line: gold if it keeps you above 20 GB, hollow if it doesn’t.

What you just saw

The answer was backwards. Every value it promised failed your test, and every value it threw out passed. But it wasn’t all wrong: 10 really is where the data hits exactly 20 GB, and 10 fails either way. The boundary was right. The side was wrong — which means one of those two steps turned the answer around, and nothing on the page said so.

The names for this, when you’re ready for them: a value that makes the inequality true is a solution, and all of them together are the solution set — here h < 10, a ray running left from 10. The 10 is the boundary: the answer to 50 − 3h = 20, the equation hiding inside the inequality. The open circle says the boundary itself isn’t included, because 20 isn’t more than 20. Solving like an equation finds the boundary. One tested number tells you which side of it is yours.

Act II The Mirror

Which step turned it around? The suspect: dividing both sides by −3.

Dividing by −3 is the same as multiplying by −⅓. Try it on something nobody argues with: 3 < 7. Multiply both sides by the same number k, and slide k from 1 down to −1. Watch the two numbers, and the sign between them.

What you just saw

Multiplying by a negative number flips the whole number line over 0, like a mirror: whatever was on the left lands on the right. 3 was left of 7; after the flip, −3 is right of −7. Every pair of numbers swaps order — so an inequality has to swap with them. Dividing by −3 is multiplying by −⅓, and it turns −3h > −30 into h < 10. That’s exactly what your tests found.

The rule, when you’re ready for it: when you multiply or divide both sides of an inequality by a negative number, reverse the inequality sign — > becomes <, ≥ becomes ≤. Adding or subtracting the same number never needs it: that slides the number line, it doesn’t flip it. Solved properly, 50 − 3h > 20 → −3h > −30 → h < 10. And test one number anyway. It takes ten seconds, and it would have caught this.

Then The Card

One question the page won’t answer for you.

The one number in between

At k = 0, the 3 and the 7 landed on the same spot, and 3 < 7 became 0 = 0.

So what happens to an inequality if you multiply both sides by zero? And once you have, could anyone get the original back?
