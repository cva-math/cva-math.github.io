IM 1/2H · Unit 2 · Can You Afford to Live Here?

A Week’s Wages

There’s an old rule for rent, older than anyone you know: a week’s wages for a month’s rent. This unit asks what that rule says about living here now, and it runs on one sentence: the answer to an affordability question is not yes or no — it is the set of lives in which the answer is yes. Nobody believes that by being told it. This page is built so you can see it — three instruments, twelve lives.

Act I The Old Rule

Pick a job. Pick a place. How many weeks of work does one month of rent cost?

A month of full-time work is about 4⅓ weeks — twenty-one and two-thirds weekdays. The old rule said a month’s rent should cost one week’s wages. Today’s line is 30% of your pay, which is 1.3 weeks. Every wage and rent below is real, public, and covers Anaheim.

What you just saw

Rent is a number of weeks of your life. The old rule gave it one. Here, on Anaheim’s own resort wage, a studio takes more than three — you’d work for the rent until the Monday of week four — and not one of the twelve lives on this page fits inside a single week.

The arithmetic, when you want it: weeks of work = rent ÷ (40 × hourly wage). A month is 52 ÷ 12 ≈ 4⅓ weeks, so 30% of it is 1.3 weeks. One week out of 4⅓ is about 23% — close to the 25% Congress wrote into law in 1969, before it moved the line to 30% in 1981.

Turn it around. Start from the rent — what’s the least you’d have to earn?

What you just saw

Every rent is secretly a wage — the least you’d have to earn to keep it inside the line. And the answer isn’t one number. It’s that wage and every wage above it: a ray, running right. Push the rent up and watch real jobs fall off the end of it, one by one.

Forward, the rule is rent ≤ 0.30 × monthly pay. Backwards, at forty hours a week, hourly wage ≥ rent ÷ 52. Why 52? Thirty percent of 40 hours times 52⁄12 weeks is exactly 52 — the most rent each dollar of hourly wage can carry.

Act III Twelve Lives

All twelve lives at once. Which ones work — and what would it take to let more of them in?

Four jobs across, three places up. The line is the rule, and every life on or under it works. You can move two things: how many hours you work a week, and where the line itself sits. Congress set it at 25% in 1969 and moved it to 30% in 1981; past 50%, HUD calls you severely cost burdened.

What you just saw

That’s the unit’s whole answer, drawn. Can you afford to live here? isn’t a yes or a no — it’s a set: every life that lands under the line. More hours tilt the line up. So does a bigger percentage. And every life you pull in, you pay for with something — the hours, a roommate, a bigger slice of every paycheck.

Every life under the line satisfies rent ≤ line × 52⁄12 × hours × wage. One inequality, two variables. Its answer isn’t a number — it’s a region, and every point in it is a life.

Coda The Part the Graph Can’t See

Every number on this page is real. None of it is the whole story.

  • Every week here is pay before taxes. That’s how the 30% rule is usually figured. The check you’d actually take home is smaller.
  • Forty hours, fifty-two weeks, no days off. It’s the standard figure, and an optimistic one — real jobs have holidays, sick days, and shifts that get cut.
  • HUD’s rent is the 40th percentile. About four in ten places here rent for less, and six in ten for more.
  • Rent isn’t the only bill. MIT’s $30.79 counts food, transportation, medical care and taxes too — and it assumes you’re sharing a place.
  • Anaheim is the default, not the rule. Every source below publishes the same numbers for your own city.
Which is the assignment

At the end of this unit you make your case — what it would take, where you’d put the line, which corner of your region you’d choose, and what it costs you — to someone who moved here, or moved away, because of what it cost. The math is the evidence. The verdict is yours.
