Act I The Shrink

On paper this house is five inches long. In the world it is twenty feet. Turn it up.

You stay the same size the whole time. Only the drawing changes — and every length in it changes by the same number at once.

Every wall got longer by the same multiplier at the same moment, so the house never went crooked. That one number is the whole trick — and it is the only thing standing between a drawing and a building.

The name for this, when you’re ready for it: the scale factor. — for this drawing it is 48, because the house is 48 times the paper, everywhere.

Act II The Grid

Nobody wrote a single measurement on this plan. Every size you need is still on it.

One grid square stands for two feet. Tap any wall and count with it — the grid is doing the translating for you.

Four different walls, one rule, every time — even the bathroom, which lands at two and a half squares and does not care that it missed the line. That is what makes it a scale rather than four separate facts.

Written the way an architect writes it: 1 square = 2 ft. — the drawing carries its own key, so anyone can read it without you standing there.

Act III The Cheat

One of these is a scaled drawing. One of them is a lie about a house. Which is which?

Both were made from the same plan. One multiplied every length by 1.5. The other used 2 on the long walls and 1.2 on the short ones — two numbers instead of one.

What you just saw

You knew before you could explain it. Your eye is already holding the rule: same number, every length. Break that and the doors stop fitting the walls, and nobody can build from the page.

The word for the good one: scaled copy. — same shape, different size. The other one has no name, because it is not a drawing of anything.

Act IV The Floor

Now scale a room instead of a wall. The sides do one thing. The floor does another.

Start with one square of floor and stretch its sides. Count the tiles that fit inside as you go — that count is the surprise of this whole unit.

What you just saw

A length rides the scale factor once. A floor rides it twice. That is exactly why a tiny house is so tiny: shrink every length of an ordinary house to a third and the floor does not fall to a third — it falls to a ninth.

The name, last as always: area scales by k². — two directions got multiplied, so the multiplier lands twice.

Act V The Ruler

Every drawing you just read was made at one setting. Here it is, written the way the trade writes it.

Half an inch on the page. Two feet in the world. Put both in the same unit before you compare them — and then just divide.

¼″ = 1′-0″
Architects say it out loud as quarter-inch scale, and write it 1:48. Same number you found in Act I — it just took the trade three hundred years to agree on how to print it.
Where this goes

By the end of the unit you will not be reading somebody else’s plan. You will pick your own scale, draw your own house at it, and hand the page to a person with a ruler — and find out whether they can read it back.

And the one you will have to answer: does it all fit? — eight feet wide, the length is your call, and everything you own is about to argue for floor space.

I can fit what matters into the space I have.
