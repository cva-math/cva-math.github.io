Twenty Plus Ten

The sign says 20% off. At the register there’s another 10% off on top. That’s 30% off — isn’t it? The receipt says otherwise.

Ring it up both ways.
Act I Ring It Up

A $50 hoodie is 20% off, with an extra 10% off at the register. Is that the same as 30% off?

Ring it up the way the store does — the sale first, then the extra 10% off whatever is left. Then ring it up as one straight 30% off. Compare what you’d pay.

What you just saw

The extra 10% didn’t come off the $50 — it came off the $40 the sale had already left. Ten percent of forty is four, not five. So the two discounts together took $14 off a $50 hoodie, and $14 out of $50 is 28%. Twenty plus ten came out to twenty-eight.

The name for this, when you’re ready for it: a discount leaves a percent of what it started with. 20% off leaves 80%, and an extra 10% off leaves 90% of that. Together, 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72 — you pay 72%, so the discount is 28%. Discounts stack by multiplying what’s left, never by adding what’s taken.

What you just saw

They only match when one of the discounts is zero. Every other pair comes out smaller than its sum — and the bigger both get, the more goes missing. 50% off and another 50% off add up to 100%, but you still pay a quarter of the price. The second discount always comes off a smaller number than the first one did.

The gap has a size. Stacking a% off and then b% off gives you a + b − a × b ÷ 100 percent off. Twenty and ten: 20 + 10 − 2 = 28. The part that goes missing is the second discount’s cut of the first one.
