The Missing Side

Multiply two polynomials and you fill in a rectangle. Divide, and someone hands you the filled-in rectangle and one side, and asks for the other. There are two ways to write that down, and they’re secretly the same.

Step through the first one. Then the second.
Act I The Missing Side

Step through it. Watch both sides light up together.

A rectangle has area x3 + 2x2 − 5x − 6, and one of its sides is x + 1. On the left, the area diagram fills in one cell at a time. On the right, long division does the same thing in lines.

The missing side, so far
—

Area x3 + 2x2 − 5x − 6  ·  one side x + 1

What you just saw

Every step happened twice — once in a box, once in a line — and it was the same step both times. Divide: what times x makes the biggest term? Multiply that by the whole side. Subtract, and see what’s still needed. The diagram keeps the pieces in boxes; long division keeps them in columns. Underneath, it’s one idea.

The name for this, when you’re ready for it: polynomial division. (x³ + 2x² − 5x − 6) ÷ (x + 1) = x² + x − 6 — the missing side. Nothing was left over, so x + 1 is a factor.

Act II When It Doesn’t Fit

Same side, slightly different area. Step through to the end.

This time the area is x3 + 2x2 − 5x − 3 — only the last number changed. The side is still x + 1.

The missing side, so far
—

Area x3 + 2x2 − 5x − 3  ·  one side x + 1

What you just saw

Everything went exactly the same until the very last subtraction — and then 3 was left over. It doesn’t fit in any box: a rectangle with sides x + 1 and x2 + x − 6 covers x3 + 2x2 − 5x − 6, and this polynomial is 3 more than that. So x + 1 goes in — with 3 to spare.

The name for this, when you’re ready for it: the remainder. x³ + 2x² − 5x − 3 = (x + 1)(x² + x − 6) + 3.

Two ways to write it down. One idea underneath.
