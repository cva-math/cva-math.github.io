From Far Enough Away

Up close, a polynomial can look like almost anything — dips, bumps, turns. From far enough away, every one of them is simple. Nobody believes that by being told it, so this page doesn’t tell you. It lets you watch.

Drag the first one. Press the second.
Act I The Zoom

Zoom out, and keep your eye on the dashed curve.

The solid curve is y = x4 − 2x3 − 7x2 + 8x + 12. Up close it’s busy — it dips, climbs, and dips again. The dashed curve is only its first piece, x4, all by itself. Right now the two look nothing alike.

What you just saw

The farther out you look, the less the middle matters. The dips and bumps never actually go away — the little window shows they’re still there. They just get too small to see next to x4, which outgrows every other term put together. From far enough away, you can’t tell this polynomial from x4.

The name for this, when you’re ready for it: x4 is the leading term — the piece with the highest power. Up close, every term matters. Out at the ends, it’s the only one that does.

What you just saw

Every middle came out different. Not one end moved. The ends belong to the leading term, and only two things about it matter: is its power even or odd, and is the number in front positive or negative? An even power sends both ends the same way. An odd power sends them opposite ways. A negative out front flips the whole picture over.

The name for this, when you’re ready for it: end behavior — which way a graph heads as x runs off to the left and to the right. The leading term decides it. The rest of the polynomial doesn’t get a vote.

Up close, a polynomial can look like anything. From far enough away, it can’t hide.
