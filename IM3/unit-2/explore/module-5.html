The Leftover

Divide a polynomial by (x − a), and there’s usually something left over. It looks like scrap. It isn’t. Slide a along the axis and catch it being something else.

Slide a along the axis. Compare the two numbers.

The curve is p(x) = x3 − 3x2 − 6x + 8. Each time you move a, the page divides p(x) by (x − a) — the same arithmetic as long division — and shows what’s left over. Separately, it measures how high the curve is at a.

What you just saw

The two numbers never disagreed. Divide by (x − a), and what’s left over is exactly the height of the curve at a — at every a you tried. So wherever the curve crosses the axis, the leftover is 0, and (x − a) goes in evenly: it’s a factor. That works both ways. A division can tell you a height, and a height of 0 tells you a factor.

The name for this, when you’re ready for it: the Remainder Theorem. Dividing p(x) by (x − a) leaves p(a) over. And when p(a) = 0, (x − a) is a factor.

Next time something’s left over, don’t throw it away.
